401(k) Basics: What That HR Form Actually Does

The bucket with a tax sticker on it.

Education only — not financial advice. ← Back to start

The one-paragraph version

A 401(k) is a type of retirement account offered through your employer. When you contribute, money is taken out of each paycheck before it ever hits your bank account, goes into this special account, and gets invested. Because the government wants to encourage you to save for retirement, it lets you skip paying tax on that money today (or, in the Roth version, pay all tax today so you never owe tax on the growth later). That’s the entire core idea. Everything else is detail.

Why “401(k)” has such a weird name

It’s not an acronym. It’s the number of a section in the U.S. tax code (Section 401, subsection k). That’s it. Some plans are called “403(b)” (nonprofits/schools) or “457” (some government jobs) — they work the same way for all practical beginner purposes. When you see one of those numbers now, you can just think “a 401(k) with a different area code.”

How the money actually moves

  1. You choose a percentage. On the HR form, you pick an amount like “6% of each paycheck.” That percentage comes out of pre-tax or Roth pay (more in Roth vs. Traditional).
  2. It never touches your normal bank account. Bypassing your checking account is a feature — you can’t spend what you never see.
  3. Your employer may add extra. This is the match — covered in detail in Employer match & vesting.
  4. It gets invested. You don’t just deposit money into a hole. It buys shares of funds (pools of many stocks/bonds). Covered in Target-date funds.
  5. It stays there for decades, (mostly) untouchable, growing tax-advantaged.

Quick math, so it feels real

Say you make $60,000/year. You pick 5%.

  • That’s $3,000/year, or about $230 taken out per biweekly paycheck.
  • If your employer matches 50 cents on the dollar up to 6%, they add another $1,500/year into your account for free.
  • Your take-home pay drops by less than $230, because pre-tax contributions also lower the tax withheld this year.
  • Neither comedy nor headlines — just this, quietly, every year, growing.

You can change your percentage later, usually whenever you want, from the plan’s website. This is not a contract written in blood at 22 forever.

Traditional vs. Roth 401(k): the sticker on the bucket

Same bucket, different tax timing. The quick version:

Traditional 401(k) Roth 401(k)
Tax when you contribute Skipped (lower taxes today) Paid in full (higher taxes today)
Tax when you withdraw in retirement Paid then $0 — growth is tax-free too
Rough rule of thumb Nicer while you’re in a high tax bracket Nicer if you expect to be in a higher bracket later

There’s a friendlier version of this in Roth vs. Traditional, including the “do both” answer nobody tells you.

What you can’t do with it (and why that’s fine)

  • Generally can’t withdraw before age 59½ without tax + a 10% penalty — there are exceptions (hardships, some loans from your own balance, etc.), but they’re the exception, not the plan. This lock-up is exactly why the tax break exists.
  • You don’t lose it if you change jobs. It goes with you as a rollover.
  • You don’t lose it if your company goes under. Your 401(k) is legally held by a separate custodian, not your employer’s bank account.

The two real beginner mistakes to avoid

  1. Cash-out on job change. Cashing out a 401(k) triggers income tax plus a 10% penalty if you’re under 59½, and zeroes out years of future growth. This single decision costs average workers five to six figures. Read Rollovers once and you’ll never do this.
  2. Not getting the full match. If your employer offers “100% match up to 4%,” every percentage you skip below 4% is a pay cut you agreed to. Details here.

Wrong fund choice, imperfect Roth-vs-Traditional guess, contributing 3% instead of 5% — none of these are as bad as those two. Give yourself credit for opening the account at all.
