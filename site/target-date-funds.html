Target-Date Funds: The “Choose Nothing” Option That’s Secretly Fine

One fund, automatically rebalances itself for your specific retirement year.

Education only — not financial advice. ← Back to start

The problem they solve

When you open a 401(k), you are handed a menu of 15 to 80 funds with names like “Value 500 Index Fund,” “International Equity II,” “SmallCap Value Clone A,” and you have absolutely no idea which to pick. You could learn to build a portfolio. Or — more practical for most people — you could pick a single fund that does it for you. That’s a target-date fund (TDF).

What a target-date fund actually is

A TDF is a fund-of-funds: it holds a mix of stock and bond funds, all inside one wrapper, and the mix automatically shifts over time.

  • The “target date” in the name = the year you plan to retire, not the year you start. A 30-year-old in 2026 targeting retirement around 2056 wants a fund labeled “Target 2055” or “Target 2060.”
  • At 2055, the mix might be ~90% stocks / 10% bonds (aggressive, since you’re far from retirement).
  • As the years pass toward the target date, it glides down to something like ~55–60% stocks / 40% bonds by ~2055 and thereafter. (The exact glide paths vary by provider. Details you can ignore for years.)

Translation: the fund gets more conservative automatically as you approach retirement, which is roughly what a sane human would do manually, minus the manual part. Aka “set and forget.”

How to pick one (in one step)

Find your likely retirement year. Pick the TDF whose label is closest to it. Done.

  • 2026 starting-out, currently 25 → you’ll likely want ~2060–2065. “Target 2060.”
  • 2026, currently 45 → ~2040–2045. “Target 2045.”
  • Already retired or 5 years out → target-date funds still exist for your year (“Target 2025” exists now, and usually keep rolling forward). Or look at what your plan calls “Retirement Income” funds. Not urgent.

You do not need to agonize. If you’re off by 5 years either direction and it still works fine. It’s the fund label, not a contract.

When TDFs are great, and when “just a TDF” is enough

Great for nearly everyone, honestly, including folks who’d enjoy manual investing more but have other things to do. It’s the financial equivalent of hiring a lawn service. It’s not “the dumb option”; it’s a fully reasonable endpoint.

Where TDFs fall short (be honest with yourself, not scared):

  • You’re saving in more than one account. A 401(k) TDF plus a separate IRA with a different mix means your combined portfolio isn’t what either fund thinks it is. Not fatal — just means your combined risk isn’t exactly “automatically managed.” Most beginners can ignore this for years.
  • You want to tweak or DIY stock/bond splits. Then use TDFs in some accounts and do your own thing elsewhere. Folks in this camp, enjoy.
  • You’re genuinely risk-averse or risk-seeking relative to your age cohort. The glide path assumes you’re an average-risk person of average age. Fine for most; a resonant few prefer custom.

The one number to look at: the expense ratio

Fees, expressed as a decimal percentage of assets per year. TDFs in good 401(k)s and at major brokerages run very cheap, often ~0.05–0.15%. Some old plans carry pricier TDFs (0.5–1.0%+). Rule of thumb over decades: going from 0.75% to 0.10% on the same balance can be worth six figures by retirement. You’re not chasing perfection here — you’re just avoiding the plain bad deals.

In your 401(k)’s fund list, look for lines like “Net Expense Ratio.” If the number before the % sign is under about 0.2 for a TDF, that’s genuinely competitive. No action needed. If you’re seeing a 401(k) TDF well over that, that’s info, not emergency — you can mention it at your next annual HR/benefits meeting.

Three misconceptions to delete from your brain

  1. “Target-date funds are risky because they’re all just stocks.” Near retirement dates, they hold plenty of bonds. Far from retirement they’re stock-heavy, which is why (over any given 10-year window) they wobble — that’s correct behavior for a 30-year-old, not a defect.
  2. “I should pick the one for when I START saving.” No. It’s for when you plan to STOP. Backwards of what the word “target” tricks you into feeling.
  3. “If I pick the wrong year I’m locked in.” You can generally switch TDFs whenever you like. It’s a fund label, not a life vow.

Do you need to retry manual investing later?

Only if you want to. Plenty of financially sophisticated people happily use TDFs for life. There’s no prize for maxing out your DIY complexity. If you ever get interested — buffer the enthusiasm through first steps, keep contributing, and treat extra tinkering as a hobby, not a rescue.
