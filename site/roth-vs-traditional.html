Roth vs. Traditional: Pay Tax Now or Pay Tax Later

The pizza-pricing version of one of retirement’s most-feared decisions.

Education only — not financial advice. ← Back to start

The 30-second answer

If you take exactly one thing from this doc: this decision is way less consequential than getting a contribution happening at all. Pick one, move on with your life. Neither choice is wrong, and you can absolutely change it next payday. But since you asked, here’s how to actually think about it.

The pizza analogy (stolen from every good teacher)

Imagine every dollar of your retirement savings is a pizza.

  • Traditional: You get a big pizza today, but the tax office slices off a piece of every future pizza when you eat it in retirement.
  • Roth: You pay for the pizza upfront, and it’s your pizza, forever, tax-free — including all the toppings (growth).

Both get you a pizza. Neither is a scam. It’s about whether you’d rather hand over a slice today at known prices, or later at unknown prices.

The actual mechanics

Traditional (the default)

  • You contribute money that hasn’t been taxed yet → lower taxable income this year.
  • Money grows tax-free inside the account.
  • You pay ordinary income tax on every withdrawal in retirement.
  • RMDs (Required Minimum Distributions) start around your mid-70s — the government makes you start withdrawing once you’re old. Annoying, mildly important, not scary.

Roth

  • You contribute money you’ve already paid tax on.
  • Money grows tax-free inside the account.
  • Retire. Withdraw. Pay nothing. Not ordinary income tax, not capital gains tax — nothing, ever, on any of it (including growth).
  • No RMDs in your lifetime. Roth IRAs are also nicer to leave to heirs than traditional accounts (though inheritance rules change; don’t over-optimize on this alone).

Rules of thumb that actually help

If you’re… Traditional usually better if
Early career, low tax bracket now You expect a higher bracket in retirement → Roth
Peak earning years (40s–50s), high bracket now Well into a high bracket → Traditional (tax break today is worth more)
Totally unsure Split it. Some of each. This is a genuinely good answer, not a cop-out.

The “just split it” argument, made honestly

You can contribute to both a Traditional 401(k) and a Roth 401(k) at the same time (most plans allow it), and to both flavors of IRA (up to a combined limit). Humans are bad at predicting future tax rates. Splitting is a mini-hedge: if taxes go up later, you were partly Roth; if they go down, you were partly Traditional. It’s boring, and it works.

Three traps worth avoiding

  1. Overthinking it to the point of not contributing. Anyone who says the “right” Roth/Traditional split is make-or-break is selling something. Contributing at 4% vs 6% matters far more to your end balance than Traditional vs Roth.
  2. Roth conversion edge cases. Sometimes people say “just convert old traditional money into Roth!” Useful tool, but not free — you pay tax on converted amounts in the same year. Do that with a tax pro or after reading 10 more pages.
  3. Assuming you’ll be in a low bracket in retirement. Many retirees’ income (Social Security, RMDs, side gigs) surprises them by not being as low as they assumed. This is part of why “some of each” exists.

Where to label this in your own head

Honestly, you don’t need to be able to explain Roth vs Traditional at a dinner party. You just need to know:

  • You picked one (or both), and that’s valid.
  • The difference is when you paid tax, not whether.
  • Your choice is reversible on your next contribution, not your last.
