Employer Match & Vesting: Free Money, With a Timer On It

Why the first 4% is the best money you will ever earn.

Education only — not financial advice. ← Back to start

The match, in one sentence

An employer match means your company adds money to your 401(k) because you did — usually a percentage of what you put in, up to a cap. It’s compensation, part of your total pay package, that only exists to reward you for contributing.

Match formulas, translated from HR

You’ll see things like these on your benefits page. Here’s what they mean in dollars, assuming a $60,000 salary:

HR says Translation Free money at full ~$60k
“100% match on the first 3% of your salary” You put in 3%, they add another 3%. $1,800/yr
“50% match up to 6%” You put in 6%, they add 3%. (Same as above, phrased differently!) $1,800/yr
“100% match up to 4%, then 50% match on the next 2%” You put in 6%, they add 5% total. $3,000/yr
“Dollar-for-dollar, up to $2,000/year” They add $1 for every $1 you contribute, until they’ve added $2,000. $2,000/yr

Translation rule of thumb: ignore the percentages and compute the dollar amount. “50% up to 6%” = “max free money is 3% of salary.” That’s the number to aim for.

The one thing to burn into memory

If your employer offers a match, getting all of it should be your financial priority #1 — before aggressively attacking low-interest debt, before fancy investing strategies, before most other goals. No other low-effort move returns a guaranteed instant 50–100%. There is literally nothing else like it available to ordinary people with normal jobs.

Vesting: when the match becomes legally yours

Here’s the catch. The match money might not be yours on day one. Vesting is the schedule by which your employer’s contributions become permanently yours if you leave.

Your own contributions are always 100% yours. Instantly. Every dollar you put in, plus whatever you earned (or lost) from investing it. Nobody can ever take that back. Only the match money has a timer on it.

Common vesting schedules

Schedule type How it works If you leave after 2 years
Immediate All match is yours the moment it’s contributed You keep 100%
Cliff (e.g., 3-year) You keep nothing until year 3, then all of it 0% (ouch)
Graded (e.g., 20% per year) Year 1: 20% yours, year 2: 40%, … year 5: 100% 40%

Also note: your company just has to tell you. It’s on the plan documents or in your online account (“Vesting service of 2.0 years,” etc.). If you can’t find yours, that’s a great question to email HR: “Could you point me to my plan’s vesting schedule?”

Real-world implications

  • Think twice about quitting in a cliff-vest window if you’re close to the edge. A 3-year cliff at 2 years 11 months is very nearly $X for one more month.
  • Job changes are normal, vests are not guilt — sometimes you just have to leave. But knowing today that your match vests at year 3 means you can make a calm decision with full information.
  • Some plans vest you immediately on legally mandated events — plan termination, or hitting retirement age per the plan rules. Nice to know, not worth waiting for.

The match is not one decision per paycheck

A common confusion: people think “the match gets funded on the first paycheck of the year” (true for some plans) or “my match stops if I max out my 401(k) early in the year” (sometimes true! Some plans calculate match per paycheck, so front-loading your contributions means later paychecks have nothing to match).

If you’re a high saver/do-it-yourselfer, ask your plan: “Is the match calculated per-paycheck or annually, and does it have a true-up at year-end?” If you’re a normal saver at a steady percentage — this doesn’t affect you. Note it, move on.

“But I can’t afford to contribute!”

Totally understandable, and common. Two honest framings:

  1. You’re not actually choosing between “take home $230” and “$0.” Because pre-tax contributions reduce your taxes today, taking home drops by less. Often 10–20% less.
  2. You’re allowed to start small. 1% is allowed. 2% is allowed. Amounts are adjustable any time — many people set a rule like “raise my contribution 1% every time I get a raise” and quietly end up at 10%+ within three years without ever feeling the squeeze.

The one genuinely bad answer is 0% with a matching employer, forever.
