# Rollovers: Moving Old 401(k)s Without Tripping Any Wires

*That account from the job you left in 2021? Yeah, let's deal with it.*

**Education only — not financial advice.** [← Back to start](index.md)

## Why this doc exists

The average American changes jobs every ~4 years and many end up with a string of forgotten 401(k)s pinned to former employers. Left alone, these accounts:

- get charged higher fees every year than you'd pay in a well-chosen IRA (variety warning: *some* employer plans are actually cheap — check yours),
- can't be contributed to anymore,
- and quietly make your whole retirement picture harder to see.

Also, the single most expensive mistake in this whole guide is what people do with them instead: **cashing out.** Cash-out = ordinary income tax + a 10% penalty (if under 59½) + permanently losing decades of tax-free compounding. On a $30,000 balance that's often $10,000+ gone, right then, on the thing you needed *least*.

## The four legitimate things you can do

### 1. Leave it (sometime—no rush)

- Pros: zero effort; some old plans have genuinely cheap funds; creditor protection under ERISA (your employer's plan is quite protected).
- Cons: forgotten accounts with bad fund menus; you can't contribute to it; small balances (<$7,000 in many cases) can be *forcibly* rolled out of an old employer plan anyway.

**Reasonable choice if:** your old plan is good, cheap, one more account doesn't stress you, and you're the rare person who's on top of it. Coming here because you *feel* disorganized? Roll it over. Your future self will thank you.

### 2. Roll it into your *new* employer's 401(k)

- Pros: one account, one login, strong creditor protection, keeps traditional-401(k) tax treatment intact.
- Cons: fund choices limited to what your new plan offers; you have to actually initiate this and it might be a phone call or a form.

**Reasonable choice if:** your new plan is decent and you want *one* login.

### 3. Roll it into an IRA (the most common & flexible)

- Pros: any brokerage, any funds you want, the whole universe of low-cost index funds, one statement per household, no RMDs until the IRS-required age (you can consolidate *multiple* old 401(k)s into one).
- Cons: you now own the "pick your own funds" responsibility (mitigated by [target-date funds](target-date-funds.md)); slightly different creditor protection than a 401(k) (state-dependent, usually still strong).

**Reasonable choice if:** you want control and simplicity. This is the default recommendation most fiduciary-style financial writers give, and this guide agrees as general education.

### 4. Roll into your current employer's plan or an IRA — *not* to your bank account

Honestly this is the same advice as the top of this doc repeated as a headline: **never cash out early if you can possibly avoid it.** That's the whole point of this page.

## "Direct" vs. "60-day" — the one distinction that matters

| | **Direct rollover** (aka trustee-to-trustee) | **60-day rollover** (indirect) |
|---|---|---|
| How it works | Old plan wires money straight to new account | Old plan mails a check *to you* |
| Risk | None — money never passes through your hands | You have **60 days** to redeposit it, or it's a taxable distribution |
| 20% withholding | No withholding | Old plan withholds 20% for taxes — you must fund it from your own pocket to make the full rollover work |
| Once per year? | Unlimited | Only **one** 60-day rollover per 12 months, per person |
| Beginner verdict | **Always ask for this** | Do not attempt unless a pro's helping you |

**In real life this means:** when you call or email the old plan, the magic phrase is *"I'd like to request a direct rollover."* You will fill out a form (or a page online), specify the receiving institution, and the money moves between institutions without a dime ever hitting your personal checking account. Done correctly, there is no tax event, no penalty, no 1099 problem, no drama.

## The step-by-step, assuming you're rolling into an IRA

1. **Find the old account.** Look through old email for the plan provider's name (Fidelity, Vanguard, Empower, etc.) — or ask your former HR. If the account's lost entirely, the [free, official search](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/ebsa/about-ebsa/our-activities/resource-center/faqs/abandoned-plans) via the Dept. of Labor can help trace it.
2. **Don't cash it out.** Seriously. See [401(k) basics](401k-basics.md) for the math.
3. **Choose your destination.** See options above. (IRA is the most common.)
4. **Open the destination account** if needed — takes about 10 minutes at any major brokerage.
5. **Request the direct rollover.** Usually an online form at the *old* provider: "I want to do a direct rollover to [institution] account [number]."
6. **Wait 1–2 weeks.** This is genuinely a slow-moving, boring process and that's fine.
7. **Watch for the money arriving as cash.** It will land in your new account as a "money market" or "settlement" balance.
8. **Invest it.** This is the step most people forget. See [Target-date funds](target-date-funds.md). Uninvested cash in a retirement account just... sits there for years, feeling like progress while doing nothing.
9. **Tell your new employer's 401(k), if any**, in case this affects anything on their side (it usually doesn't).
10. **File the confirmation somewhere.** You're done forever. No tax forms needed in most cases (you may get a "no taxable amount" footnote on a 1099-R — do not panic; that's normal for direct rollovers).

## Special cases, briefly

- **Roth 401(k) → Roth IRA:** Clean, tax-free move. (Traditional 401(k) → Roth IRA is a taxable *conversion* — do this thoughtfully, not on autopilot.)
- **After-tax 401(k) contributions:** If you're in a plan with after-tax contributions + mega-backdoor features, you're no longer a beginner. Congrats, and please get real advice.
- **Same-company stock inside the old plan (NUA):** A rare, real, but niche strategy. Deep, not for beginners. Get advice.
- **Rollover IRA → later new job's 401(k):** Totally allowed, sometimes useful defensively (e.g., to enable backdoor Roth later). File under things to know, not things to do now.
