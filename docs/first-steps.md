# Confident First Steps: A Calm 30-Day Checklist

*Nothing here requires research, spreadsheets, or confidence you don't have yet.*

**Education only — not financial advice.** [← Back to start](index.md)

The recurring theme: **retirement saving is not a personality trait.** It's a small number of boring actions, done once, that then run on their own. Here they are, in order, with permission to take a full month.

## Days 1–3: Just look. Don't decide anything yet.

1. **Find your plan's website.** Look in your HR emails for your plan provider's name (it's whoever runs the 401(k) portal) and your account details.
2. **Log in once.** That's it. Looking at the dashboard is a real accomplishment the first time. It's designed to feel like a bank's boring cousin, not a physics exam.
3. **Find three numbers and write them down** (this is what the [worksheet](worksheet.md) is for):
   - What percentage are you currently contributing? (If 0%, that's a fact, not a failure.)
   - Does your employer match? How much? (*"100% up to 3%"*, etc.)
   - Vesting schedule — cliff, graded, or immediate? (If unclear, email HR: *"Could you tell me my plan's vesting schedule and whether I'm fully vesting?"*)

## Days 4–10: Set your contribution percentage.

4. **Pick a number you can actually live with** — not an aspirational number, a real one. Even 1% is a valid choice. Even 6% is allowed. Common sweet spots: **enough to capture the full employer match**, or 5–10% if you intend to build a habit.
5. **Set it in the online portal.** Takes about 90 seconds. You can change it any time; nothing is permanent.
6. **Optional but good:** enable an "auto-escalation" if your plan offers it (automatically bumps your contribution by 1% each year). This is the laziest possible way to become a great saver.

If anxiety around money numbers is real for you: set a 15-minute timer, do step 5, close the laptop. Celebrate with a treat. Seriously. Progress on this front is about *starting*, not optimizing.

## Days 11–20: Decide where the money goes inside the account.

7. **Open the "Investment options" or "Fund lineup" section.** Yes, it's a wall of confusing fund names. That's normal. You're about to bypass most of it.
8. **Look for words like "Target," "Target Date," or "Lifecycle."** These are the [target-date funds](target-date-funds.md).
9. **Pick the one whose year matches your expected retirement year.** 2060 for a 25-year-old in 2026. 2045 for someone in their mid-40s. This is genuinely the whole decision.
10. **Direct your contributions** (both *current* contributions and, if asked, "[default] future contributions") into that single fund. "100% in one target-date fund" is a real, legitimate, permanent-feeling choice that many financially sophisticated people use for decades.

That's it. You now have a functioning retirement plan with a sensible global mix of stocks and bonds that rebalances itself as you age. This is, genuinely, most of the ballgame.

## Days 21–30: Cleanup and closures.

11. **Deal with old 401(k)s** if you have any wandering around. Bit-by-bit: address from old email or HR → [rollover](rollovers.md) → direct rollover form → wait 1–2 weeks → **invest the arriving cash**. (Do not skip the last step. Uninvested rollover cash is the most common rollover mistake.)
12. **Choose paper or digital statements** — whichever you'll actually read. There's no moral score here; pick the one that works for you.
13. **Add a calendar reminder**: a gentle "check my retirement account" every ~3 months, and "raise my contribution 1%" whenever you get a raise. That's the entire ongoing maintenance burden for the next several years, and it takes 10 minutes per reminder.
14. **Decide whether to do an IRA** at all this year, or just let the 401(k) ride. If you're already at the full match, next in line is usually an IRA up to its (small) annual limit, *then* back to the 401(k). If this paragraph feels heavy — skip it. The 401(k) alone is a perfectly great system.

## The order of operations, as a mantra

1. Full employer match (`401k`)
2. IRA up to its limit (`roth` picks your preference)
3. More into the 401(k) if you have room and appetite
4. Then things that are far beyond the scope of a friendly guide.

Nothing about steps 1–3 requires a spreadsheet, a math degree, or knowing what an index fund is. If you complete steps 1–3 at *any* income level, you're doing far better than the median.

## If it all feels like too much

Do *exactly* this today and consider it a win:

> Log into your 401(k), set your contribution to match your employer's maximum amount, and put 100% of it into the target-date fund with the year closest to your retirement. Close the laptop.

Everything else in this guide is elaboration on that one sentence. You can read the rest [later](index.md), including the [glossary](glossary.md) and the [fridge worksheet](worksheet.md).
