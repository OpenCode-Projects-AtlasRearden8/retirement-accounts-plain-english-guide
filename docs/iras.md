# IRAs: The Retirement Account *You* Open, Not Your Boss

*Same bucket idea, but it follows you around.*

**Education only — not financial advice.** [← Back to start](index.md)

## The one-paragraph version

An **IRA** (Individual Retirement Account) is a retirement account you open yourself, at any major brokerage, independent of your job. You fund it from your own bank account (up to an annual limit), pick your own investments, and enjoy the same traditional/Roth tax logic as 401(k)s. It's the account that makes retirement saving possible when you're self-employed, between jobs, or have a 401(k) that's all used up.

## Three kinds you'll actually encounter

| Type | What it is | One-line takeaway |
|---|---|---|
| **Traditional IRA** | Contribute pre-tax (sometimes — see below), pay tax on withdrawal. | 401(k) logic, smaller limit, no employer match. |
| **Roth IRA** | Contribute after-tax, pay $0 tax on withdrawal + growth. | The most flexible & beloved account for beginners. |
| **Rollover IRA** | A traditional IRA holding money you rolled out of an old 401(k). | A parking spot, not a separate philosophy. |

## Why the Roth IRA is quietly the best beginner account in America

Assuming you're eligible (see income limits below), the Roth IRA is the single most flexible retirement account:

1. **Tax-free forever**, including all growth. Contribute $7,000 (illustrative number — check current limits), it grows to $14,000 — that $7,000 of growth is *never* taxed if you follow the rules.
2. **You can withdraw your *contributions* — not earnings — any time, penalty-free.** This is unique. (It's still a bad idea to raid it, but the safety valve lowers the fear of "locking money away.")
3. **No RMDs in your lifetime.** You never *have to* withdraw if you don't need to.
4. **You pick the investments.** You pick the brokerage. You can move it whenever you want. It's *your* account, not tied to any employer.
5. **It lasts.** You can contribute at age 22 and it can sit there, uninterrupted, until 92.

Sounds too good? The catch is the annual contribution limit (a few thousand dollars a year — roughly a small fraction of a 401(k) limit) and income phase-outs. Which brings us to...

## Eligibility: the income limits (in one breath)

- **Roth IRA contributions phase out** as your Modified Adjusted Gross Income (MAGI) climbs past a threshold that changes yearly (low six figures for single filers, roughly $200–250k-ish for joint filers — **check the current numbers at [IRS.gov](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/amount-of-roth-ira-contributions-you-can-make-based-on-income)**). Over the full phase-out, you can't contribute directly at all.
- **Traditional IRA:** Anyone can *contribute*. But the **tax deduction** phases out if you're *also* covered by a workplace plan like a 401(k) and your income is above certain limits. If the deduction is fully phased out, your traditional IRA contribution is still allowed but taxed going in — at which point you should probably just do a Roth instead (assuming you're Roth-eligible).
- **The Backdoor Roth:** A legal workaround (contribute to a non-deductible traditional IRA, then convert it to Roth) for high earners — glance at it, then do it with a tax pro if you're really in that bracket. Not beginner homework.

## How to actually open one (10 real steps)

1. Pick a major brokerage — large, well-known, low-fee ones are all fine. (This guide doesn't pick one for you. They all do the same basic job.)
2. Choose account type: **Roth IRA** (most common for beginners) or Traditional.
3. Have your routing/bank account handy for the initial transfer.
4. Expect questions about your employment, income, and investment experience. Answer honestly.
5. Open it — under 20 minutes online, usually.
6. Transfer in an amount you're comfortable with — even $50 to start.
7. **The step people miss:** money arrives as *cash* ("settlement fund" / "money market"). It earns nearly nothing until you actually *invest* it. See [Target-date funds](target-date-funds.md) for the low-fuss option.
8. Most brokerages have an "automatic investing" toggle. Turn it on. This makes contributions auto-invest, and you can forget it.
9. You can set up automatic monthly deposits. Small but consistent beats large but seasonal.
10. Done. Check it maybe once a month at first, then less.

## Common beginner questions

- **Can I have an IRA *and* a 401(k)?** Yes. Separate limits, separate rules.
- **Can I withdraw from it if it's my only emergency fund?** Roth *contributions* can come out penalty-free and tax-free (with rules). Traditional and Roth *earnings* generally can't (before 59½) without tax + penalty. An IRA is not truly an emergency fund — but Roth at least parses to "emergency valve exists."
- **What if I make too much for a Roth this year?** Note it, do Traditional (even without the deduction, it still grows tax-free inside the account), or look into the backdoor Roth when ready.
- **Is my IRA FDIC-insured?** Not exactly — FDIC protects bank accounts, not investments. If you're at a real SIPC-member brokerage, your *securities* are protected against brokerage failure (though not against market losses, which are not a failure — that's just investing).
